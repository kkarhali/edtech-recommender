## 1.1 Introduction

Modern web technology has changed how higher education is delivered, and e-learning is now a common way to offer distance learning and to give students access to content at any time and from any place. The approved proposal observes that many institutions still struggle to give students a good learning experience in this setting, and it proposes a recommendation system that uses an analysis of user behaviour to give each student personal suggestions based on individual needs and interests. A recommendation system of this kind studies what a learner has opened, in what order and how often, and uses that record to rank the learning resources the learner has not yet used.

Three families of method serve this purpose, and chapter 2 reviews them. Content-based filtering (CBF) recommends resources similar to those a learner has used, judging similarity by resource features. Collaborative filtering (CF) recommends resources used by learners with similar histories. A hybrid recommendation system combines them, with the aim of keeping the strengths of each. A learner with little or no history, a cold-start learner, is the case in which single techniques are usually reported to fail, and the hypothesis of this thesis (section 1.6.1) names it explicitly.

The proposal argues that a behaviour-based recommender could change e-learning considerably, by improving the fit of materials to students, their engagement and their results. This thesis treats that argument as motivation and not as a result. It does not test whether any recommender changes what learners learn, because an offline comparison of ranking models on logged data cannot show that (chapter 3, section 3.1). What the thesis tests is narrower and can be checked against files on disk: whether a hybrid that blends content-based and collaborative scores ranks the resources a learner later opened better than either technique alone, for established learners and for new ones, across every module presentation of a public dataset, under criteria written down before the experiment ran.

## 1.2 Scientific problem and justification

### 1.2.1 The problem in the proposal's terms

The proposal states the problem as follows. In e-learning, students face many choices among learning materials that differ in level, quality and teaching style, and are often unsure which materials will serve them best. They also have little direct contact with teachers and peers, so they receive no immediate personal guidance. The need that follows is for an effective recommendation system that analyses user behaviour, that is, the browsing history and the interaction with materials, and turns it into guidance for each student. The scientific problem is therefore how to design a recommendation system for e-learning that is based on user behaviour analysis, in order to improve the learning experience and the effectiveness of e-learning.

### 1.2.2 Why the problem is a research problem and not only an engineering task

Three findings of the literature review (chapter 2) make the design question open and not settled by the existing hybrid literature.

First, within the sources that could be opened, e-learning recommenders are evaluated mainly with accuracy-type or satisfaction measures on small, private or crawled samples, and none of those sources evaluates a behaviour-aware hybrid on a public learner clickstream with a separate cold-start slice and replication across course presentations (chapter 2, section 2.9). The claim is about missing evidence in the sources opened, and chapter 2 names the unopened items that could defeat it.

Second, a hybrid is not automatically better than the technique it combines. The reviewed literature contains a hybrid that is less accurate than pure collaborative filtering and a study in which collaborative filtering was the best single model (chapter 2, section 2.9). A hybrid must therefore be compared against the stronger of its components and not only the weaker, and this thesis does so in every criterion.

Third, the evaluation practice of the field is uneven. Chapter 2 reports that none of the proposal's nine papers gives repeated-run variance or a significance test in the text the scholar read, and that current evaluation guidance asks for splits that follow time, for strong and non-personalised baselines, and for a stated test unit (chapter 2, section 2.6). The thesis is designed to meet each of those requirements (chapter 3), and the leakage-related ones are checked by counted assertions (chapter 4).

A fourth point comes from the project's own history and is stated because it shapes the design. Five of the nine papers in the proposal are summarised in the proposal in a way that differs from what the papers themselves report: the song-recommendation recurrent network, the adaptive ensemble AMRE, the random-plus-nearest-neighbour hybrid, the keyword-to-URL classifier and the convolutional course recommender. <!-- C1.1 --> Chapter 2 records each difference, and the supervisor should see them before submission. The consequence for this thesis is that no numeric target is taken from those papers: their numbers describe other domains and other tasks.

### 1.2.3 The problem as this thesis states it

Given logged interactions between learners and learning resources in a virtual learning environment, does a hybrid recommender that blends content-based scores, collaborative scores and a behavioural engagement signal rank the resources a learner later uses higher than content-based filtering alone and higher than collaborative filtering alone, for learners with an established history (the warm slice) and for learners with almost none (the cold-start slice)? The measure is Precision@10 on held-out later interactions. The question is asked separately for each of the 22 module presentations of the dataset, and the answer is judged over all 22 with a significance test. <!-- C1.31 --> That is a question about ranking accuracy on logs; the proposal's wider aim of a better learning experience is the reason for asking it and is not what is measured.

### 1.3.3 Scope decisions, with their reasons

Four things in the proposal, the plan or the frozen hypothesis are not delivered as written. Each is stated here with its reason, who decided it and what follows from it. None changes the hypothesis, a success criterion or a research question, which stay as fixed before the experiment ran.

**1. The user interface and the related software are deferred, not dropped.** The proposal promises an easy-to-use interface as part of the system. No module of the plan builds one. <!-- C1.3 --> The candidate decided on 2026-09-20 that this is a scope-out that is deferred and not dropped, and that chapter 1 states it with its reason and chapter 6 lists it as future work (`decisions.log`, event Q5). The reason is that the experiment defined by the frozen idea and the plan is an offline comparison of ranking models with ranking metrics as its criteria, and that the design recruits no participants (chapter 3, section 3.10), so an interface could not have been evaluated by any measurement this thesis makes. The consequence is that the thesis delivers a recommendation model and a reproducible pipeline that evaluates it, not a system a learner can use. The plan records that dropping the interface formally needs the supervisor's sign-off; whether the supervisor accepts the deferral is not yet known and is listed as an open question.

**2. The deep models (RNN, CNN, RBFNN) and TensorFlow or Keras are deferred, not dropped.** The proposal names recurrent, convolutional and radial-basis-function networks among its keywords and lists TensorFlow and Keras among the proposed technologies. <!-- C1.4 --> The plan marks them out of scope for this phase because the frozen idea reserves deep learning for a later phase and criterion C5 bounds the compute to a single CPU with no GPU and no downloaded model weights. The candidate's decision of 2026-09-20 is the same as for the interface: deferred, and listed as future work in chapter 6. The reason is compute and scope, not a finding about the models. The consequence is that the collaborative component of the hybrid is classical item-based cosine collaborative filtering, and nothing in this thesis says whether a deep model would rank better or worse. Chapter 4 states that TensorFlow and Keras were not used.

**3. Dwell time does not exist in the data, so click volume, active days and click sequence stand in for it.** The hypothesis names a behavioural session signal of "browsing sequence, dwell time, engagement pattern". The one dataset used, OULAD, records for each learner, resource and day only a day offset and a click count, and holds no field for time on page or session length. <!-- C1.5 --> The candidate decided on 2026-09-21 that click volume per learner and resource, the count of active days and the day-ordered click sequence are the behavioural proxy, that this is a recorded scope reduction, and that the hypothesis stays frozen (`decisions.log`, event Q2). The proxy is thinner than the signal the hypothesis describes, because a click count cannot tell a resource opened by accident from one studied for an hour. The phrase "dwell time" is therefore never used in this thesis for anything that was measured, and every statement about the behavioural signal means the proxy.

**4. Research question RQ2, as the plan words it, cannot be answered by this design.** RQ2 asks whether segmenting learners by behavioural profile, by clustering over the Module D features, improves the cold-start prior over a non-segmented hybrid. The plan says that Module D feeds Module B with that prior. <!-- C1.6 --> The G4 code review found that the protocol compares six models and that none of them is segmented, so the comparison RQ2 needs, a segmented hybrid against a non-segmented one, is never run (`review.md`). <!-- C1.28 --> Module D was built as a descriptive module that clusters the learners of each presentation with K-Means and feeds no model, and a test in the code checks that no model-facing module refers to it (`code/behaviour.py`, `code/tests/test_evaluate.py`). The reason is a mismatch between the plan's wording of the question and the design that the protocol then fixed, found before the main run and not corrected then, because a change of research question goes to the human (integrity rule 8). RQ2 is therefore reported in chapter 5 as unanswerable from this run, and chapter 6 says so. What Module D does deliver is a description of the behavioural features and of the learner segments in each presentation, which is what RQ1 uses. Whether a descriptive analysis is enough to answer RQ1 in full is a question chapter 5 has to answer for itself.

### 1.3.4 What the pre-registered criteria can and cannot test

The hypothesis has three components: a content-based one, a collaborative one and a behavioural one. Criteria C1 to C3 compare the hybrid with content-based filtering alone, with collaborative filtering alone and with a popularity baseline, on the warm slice (C1) and on the cold-start slice (C2 and C3). No criterion compares the hybrid with and without its behavioural signal. The behavioural clause of the hypothesis is therefore tested only in the sense that the hybrid, which uses the proxy, is compared with the single techniques; it is not tested on its own terms, and where a later chapter reports a diagnostic on the behavioural features it says that the diagnostic decides no criterion. This is a property of the criteria as they were fixed, and it is stated here so that no later chapter can be read as having tested more than it did.

Four further terms are used from here on. A *learner* is a student in one module presentation. A *module presentation* is one run of one module, such as EEE_2014J, the presentation of module EEE that began in October 2014; the dataset has 22 of them. <!-- C1.16 --> A *resource* is one item of the virtual learning environment. ### 1.6.1 The hypothesis

The hypothesis is frozen and bound to the idea file of the project (binding `918162da54511db3`). <!-- C1.8 -->

> A hybrid recommender that combines (a) content-based filtering over course/content metadata, (b) collaborative filtering over learner-content interaction history, and (c) a behavioral session signal (browsing sequence, dwell time, engagement pattern, as used for student profiling in the reviewed literature) will produce more accurate and more robust recommendations for e-learning content than either content-based or collaborative filtering alone, and will specifically outperform them on cold-start learners (users with very little interaction history), which the reviewed hybrid baselines identify as the main failure mode of single-technique approaches.

The hypothesis is refutable in exactly the terms of C1 and C2: if the hybrid does not beat both baselines by at least 10% relative on warm users, or does not beat both on new learners, it is not supported. <!-- C1.30 --> The clause "dwell time" is read through the proxy of section 1.3.3, item 3.

### 1.6.2 The research questions

The five questions, with the pointers to modules and criteria that the plan attaches to them, are as follows. <!-- C1.9 -->

- **RQ0 (main):** Does a hybrid of content-based filtering, collaborative filtering and a behavioural engagement signal produce more accurate and more robust top-K e-learning recommendations than either alone, and on cold-start learners? Modules B, C; C1-C3; chapter 5.
- **RQ1 (sub-objective 1, behaviour analysis):** Which observable VLE behaviours (click volume, active-day count, day-ordered sequence, activity type) separate learners well enough to carry recommendation signal? Answered by Module D, chapters 3 and 5.

### 1.6.4 The design in brief

Chapter 3 gives the design in full; this paragraph gives only what a reader needs to follow the rest of this chapter. The experiment uses the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD), which the authors publish under CC BY 4.0. <!-- C1.29 --> It holds 22 module presentations of seven modules and 32,593 learner rows in `studentInfo.csv`. <!-- C1.16 --> After two learner filters (at least five distinct resources and at least three distinct first-interaction days), 27,212 learners are evaluated across the 22 presentations, and 997 learners who opened few resources on one or two days are excluded by the second filter. <!-- C1.17 --> Six models are compared on byte-identical splits: a random floor, global popularity, week-aware popularity, content-based filtering, item-based collaborative filtering and the hybrid; four of them are the models the plan names. <!-- C1.18 --> Each learner's history is split by day, so that for every learner the latest training or validation day is strictly earlier than the earliest test day. The cold-start slice is built by refitting every learner-dependent fitted quantity without the held-out learners, so that the model that ranks for a new learner has not been shaped by that learner. The primary measure is Precision@10, compared per presentation and judged over the 22 presentations with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test corrected by the Holm method. <!-- C1.31 --> Everything ran on one machine without a GPU. The population is UK Open University distance learners in 2013 and 2014 and not the candidate's institution, <!-- C1.16 --> and the evaluated learners are the active ones; both limits travel with every result.

### 1.7.1 What the thesis found, in brief

The findings are stated here only as a preview; chapter 5 holds the tables, the per-presentation values and the reasoning, and chapter 6 the answers to the questions. Every value is a median over the 22 module presentations, and every sentence names its baseline.

On the warm slice, criterion C1 is met: the hybrid's Precision@10 is higher than that of content-based filtering by a median relative gain of +31.20% and higher than that of collaborative filtering by a median relative gain of +10.68%, and both comparisons have a Holm-corrected paired Wilcoxon p below 0.05 (0.011857 and 0.014095). <!-- C1.19 --> The comparison with collaborative filtering clears its threshold of +10% by 0.68 percentage points, so C1 is met without a wide margin. <!-- C1.20 -->

On the cold-start slice built by refitting, criteria C2 and C3 are not met. Against collaborative filtering the hybrid has a median relative gain of +7.32%, but against content-based filtering it has a median relative gain of -30.28%, and C2 requires a positive gain against both. <!-- C1.21 --> Read against the random ranker, an exploratory and post-hoc comparison of the same scored results that decides no criterion, the median cold-start Precision@10 is 0.0728 for content-based filtering, 0.0484 for the random ranker, 0.0473 for the hybrid, 0.0377 for collaborative filtering and 0.0266 for global popularity; collaborative filtering beats the random ranker in 6 of the 22 presentations and the hybrid in 10 of 22, so only content-based filtering clearly beats chance on this slice and being ahead of collaborative filtering there is a low bar. <!-- C1.32 --> C3 holds, in all four conjuncts, in 2 of the 22 presentations. <!-- C1.22 --> Under the primary split, the receipted run inverts the reading of the phase 1 pilot, which had the hybrid ahead of content-based filtering and behind collaborative filtering on cold start in all 22 presentations; a further contrast (chapter 5, section 5.4.1, Table 5.14) shows that inversion's direction tracks the evaluation split rather than the cold-start construction, and the pilot's own reading is prior evidence, reproduced under a receipt for one presentation only (chapter 4, section 4.4). <!-- C1.23 -->

The integrity and feasibility criteria are met. C4 finds zero rows shared between the validation and test splits and the test split scored at most once per model. <!-- C1.24 --> C5 finds a main run of 1.014 CPU-hours against a budget of 4.0, and a reproduction inside its limit of 15 minutes; the verdict is combined by hand from the two receipts, because each stage's own verdict file reads "not met (unchecked conjunct)" for the conjunct it cannot see. <!-- C1.25 --> C6 finds all six reproduced phase 1 values equal to the recorded ones. <!-- C1.26 --> The hypothesis is therefore supported on the warm slice and not supported on the cold-start slice, where the hybrid's median Precision@10 is above that of collaborative filtering and below that of content-based filtering, and the criterion as written requires it to be above both.

## 1.9 What this chapter established

This chapter established the question of the thesis (whether a hybrid recommender beats content-based and collaborative filtering for established and for new learners on public e-learning logs), the hypothesis, the five research questions and the six success criteria in the words in which the approved plan fixed them, and the four sub-objectives of the proposal with the way each is served. It established four scope decisions: the user interface and the deep models are deferred and not dropped, dwell time is replaced by a declared click-based proxy, and RQ2 as the plan words it cannot be answered by this design. It previewed the outcome: C1 met, C2 and C3 not met, C4 to C6 met, each with its baseline named. <!-- C1.27 --> It did not establish any result beyond that preview, and it did not settle four things that the candidate and the supervisor must decide: the language, citation style and format that the university's regulations require, the wording of the AI-assistance disclosure, the supervisor's acceptance of the two deferred scope-outs, and whether the wording of RQ2 is to be changed. Chapter 2 uses from this chapter the terms of section 1.5, the hypothesis and the questions of section 1.6, and the declared proxy for the behavioural signal, and it must end in a gap that the criteria of section 1.6.3 can test.

## 2.0 Purpose of this chapter

This chapter establishes what the literature says about recommending learning content from learner behaviour, how that literature was found, where its evidence is thin, and which gap the experiments in this thesis address. It fixes the terms used in every later chapter (section 2.1), reviews the field by theme (sections 2.2 to 2.7), compares the nine papers reviewed in the approved proposal with later peer-reviewed work (section 2.8) and closes with the gap (section 2.9). The search protocol, query log, screening counts and the sources that could not be opened are in `literature/search_log.md`; the review is PRISMA-style lite <!-- ledger: page2021prisma --> and uses backward and forward snowballing <!-- ledger: wohlin2014snowballing -->. <!-- C2.1 --> It is organised by concept and not paper by paper <!-- ledger: webster2002review -->. <!-- C2.2 -->

Trade press is used only to frame the problem. Journalists argue that recommenders shape how people choose and see themselves <!-- ledger: schrage2022recommender -->, that AI can personalise education and narrow equity gaps <!-- ledger: castro2023spectrum -->, and that claims for AI in classrooms may run ahead of independent evidence <!-- ledger: odonnell2025classroom -->; vector databases are named as an industrial substrate for similarity search <!-- ledger: bergstein2022vector -->. <!-- C2.3 --> These four items report no reproducible method or metric and carry no weight in what follows. Two peer-reviewed Communications of the ACM articles named in the knowledge base <!-- ledger: furini2022twins --> <!-- ledger: mauro2022autism --> could not be opened (publisher returned HTTP 403 on every attempt); nothing in this chapter relies on them. <!-- C2.4 -->

## 2.2 Recommender foundations

Content-based filtering (CBF) recommends items similar to those a user liked, using item features; collaborative filtering (CF) recommends items liked by users with similar histories; hybrids combine such techniques to improve performance <!-- ledger: burke2002hybrid -->. <!-- C2.8 --> Burke's survey of hybrid designs is the standard reference for the combinations <!-- ledger: burke2002hybrid -->. <!-- C2.9 --> A current systematic review lists scalability, cold start and sparsity as recurring limitations of recommenders <!-- ledger: roy2022review -->. <!-- C2.10 --> Item-based CF, which derives item-item relationships from the user-item matrix and recommends from them, was reported to give dramatically better results than user-based CF in its original evaluation <!-- ledger: sarwar2001itembased -->. <!-- C2.11 --> A review of content-based and context-aware recommenders exists for the context-aware family named in the proposal, but the item found for it was excluded because its abstract states no method or evaluation (search log, full-text exclusions); the context-aware family is therefore thinly sourced here and is not part of the experiments.

The approved proposal's hybrids differ in kind. One combines behavioural user profiles with CF and is less accurate than pure CF on all three accuracy measures reported, its advantage being computation time <!-- ledger: degemmis2004hybrid -->. <!-- C2.12 --> One combines random recommendation with nearest neighbours in an interactive loop and is compared with a random recommender, not with a CBF-CF hybrid <!-- ledger: zhang2015hybrid -->. <!-- C2.13 --> One selects among recommenders adaptively <!-- ledger: huang2019amre -->. <!-- C2.14 --> One is an architecture with a preliminary test on 12 users <!-- ledger: ansari2016coders -->. <!-- C2.15 -->

What this means here. This thesis takes item-based CF and CBF as the two single-technique baselines and a learned blend as its hybrid; the literature it rests on says that a hybrid is not automatically better than pure CF, so the comparison must be made against the stronger baseline and a non-personalised popularity baseline.

## 2.3 Learner behaviour analytics and student profiling

Student profiles in the reviewed literature combine age band, behaviour type and behaviour duration, with similarity computed by the Jaccard coefficient <!-- ledger: liang2017profile -->. <!-- C2.16 --> A survey of machine-learning techniques for student profiles finds that academic, personal identity and online behaviour characteristics dominate <!-- ledger: hamim2021profile -->. <!-- C2.17 --> A 2024 review of course recommenders reports growing use of multi-dimensional learner profiles and a lack of attention to learners' micro-behaviours and learning actions <!-- ledger: narimani2024profiling -->. <!-- C2.18 --> On the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD), a learning-performance predictor built on behaviours grouped by learning process was reported to beat traditional classification methods (abstract-level reading) <!-- ledger: qiu2022behaviour -->. <!-- C2.19 --> In a limited-information platform, implicit ratings were inferred from counts and temporal analysis of sessions in logs <!-- ledger: sanguino2022course -->. <!-- C2.20 -->

OULAD holds 22 module-presentations, 32,593 students and logs of interactions in the virtual learning environment as daily summaries of clicks (10,655,280 entries) <!-- ledger: kuzilek2017oulad -->. <!-- C2.21 --> Its data descriptor states a CC BY 4.0 licence. <!-- C2.22 --> That licence was unverified when this chapter was drafted (2026-09-20), as the plan then recorded; it has since been verified as CC BY 4.0 by the Methodologist at stage S3, whose verification is reported in section 3.2 of chapter 3 and section 2 of the data statement (methodology/data_statement.md) and is not restated here. <!-- C2.23 --> The residual gap those sections name is that the local `oulad.zip` archive is not traced to the authors' deposit.

What this means here. OULAD supplies click volume, active days and day order per learner and resource. The profiling papers use duration, which OULAD lacks, so the behavioural signal in this thesis is a reduced version of theirs and is never called dwell time. <!-- C2.24 -->

## 2.4 Sequence and neural methods

Session-based recommenders capture short-term dynamic preferences, where CBF and CF usually model long-term static ones <!-- ledger: wang2021session -->. <!-- C2.25 --> The RNN of Hidasi et al. for sessions used ranking losses and was evaluated primarily with Recall@20 and MRR@20 <!-- ledger: hidasi2016gru4rec -->. <!-- C2.26 --> The proposal's RNN paper applies the idea to songs, holding out each user's last session; its RNN beats popularity and two nearest-neighbour baselines on Precision@N but not the session-kNN variant V-SKNN (Precision@5 of 0.095 against 0.195) <!-- ledger: kumar2023session -->. <!-- C2.27 --> Its authors themselves remark that nearest-neighbour methods have proved competitive with neural methods <!-- ledger: kumar2023session -->, in line with a reproducibility study in which 11 of 12 reproducible neural approaches were outperformed by simple methods such as nearest neighbours or linear models <!-- ledger: ferrari2021troubling -->. <!-- C2.28 -->

For e-learning, a convolutional sequence-embedding model over course sequences reported Precision@5 of 0.39 against 0.23 and 0.26 for two baselines on 500 crawled learners; its abstract claims a lower course dropout rate, but the experiments score truancy (mis-selected course) prediction, so no dropout result is measured <!-- ledger: gao2022cnn -->; a 2022 review covers MLP, RNN, CNN, attention and reinforcement-learning course recommenders <!-- ledger: liu2022dlelearning -->. <!-- C2.29 --> The proposal's RBFNN paper fuses evidence and an RBF network for e-commerce on 120 samples, with results shown only in figures <!-- ledger: sabitha2022uirs -->; a later RBF-network CF method addresses sparsity and new-user cold start on benchmark data (abstract-level reading) <!-- ledger: mohammadi2022rbf -->. <!-- C2.30 -->

What this means here. Phase 2 has a single-CPU budget and no deep model. The deep-sequence literature is reviewed because the proposal names it, but RNN, CNN and RBFNN are reported as reserved for later work and not as baselines.

## 2.5 E-learning and MOOC recommenders

Reviews agree on the shape of the field. Hybrid was the leading strategy among 16 primary studies (2015 to 2020), and evaluation was mainly on accuracy, with few studies of pedagogical effectiveness (abstract-level) <!-- ledger: dasilva2023educrs -->. <!-- C2.31 --> A review of 51 studies to mid-2021 found explicit feedback and ratings most frequent in empirical work <!-- ledger: souabi2021elearning -->. <!-- C2.32 --> Cold-start work in e-learning adds learner characteristics: an ontology-based hybrid for the new-user problem (abstract-level) <!-- ledger: jeevamol2021ontology -->, and an attribute-based hybrid evaluated by a user test with 82 percent satisfaction for recommended materials <!-- ledger: butmeh2024coldstart -->. <!-- C2.33 --> The closest analogue to this thesis is a course hybrid built from Google Analytics session logs of an online platform, where CF was the best model at P@5 of 0.4 and content-based strategies did not yield significant results but helped mitigate cold start <!-- ledger: sanguino2022course -->. <!-- C2.34 --> The proposal's own e-learning papers are a keyword-to-URL classifier with 98.98 percent accuracy <!-- ledger: chaudhary2019elearning --> and a platform architecture tried on 12 users <!-- ledger: ansari2016coders -->. <!-- C2.35 -->

What this means here. E-learning evidence comes from small, private or crawled samples and from satisfaction or accuracy measures. Nothing in the opened sources reports top-K precision for a hybrid on a public learner clickstream with a separate cold-start slice.

## 2.6 Offline evaluation practice

Offline evaluation is an essential complement to online tests, and data partitioning (random or temporal, global or per-user, split ratio) is a configuration choice that needs care <!-- ledger: castells2022offline -->. <!-- C2.36 --> A split that does not follow the global timeline lets models learn from future interactions and can change the relative order of models <!-- ledger: ji2023leakage -->. <!-- C2.37 --> A reproducibility study compared neural methods with a consistent set of simple baselines that includes a non-personalised most-popular recommender, which is why the comparison here includes such baselines <!-- ledger: ferrari2021troubling -->. <!-- C2.38 --> For significance testing, an empirical study found that Wilcoxon and sign tests show higher Type I error than bootstrap, randomisation or t-tests at large sample sizes and recommends reporting effect size <!-- ledger: ihemelandu2023inference -->. <!-- C2.39 --> In the notes taken on the nine proposal papers (`literature/notes`), none records repeated-run variance or a significance test; the notes on Zhang et al. and the ledger row on Gao et al. state the absence explicitly, and for the other papers it is inferred from the notes' silence and was not checked by a further full-text search; the splits are, per source: each user's last session held out <!-- ledger: kumar2023session -->; a 70:30 train/test split of a keyword-by-URL matrix <!-- ledger: chaudhary2019elearning -->; a random 3:1:1 train/validation/test split over samples, not per user <!-- ledger: liang2017profile -->; and random withholding of 25 percent of each test user's items after a 90/10 user split <!-- ledger: degemmis2004hybrid -->. <!-- C2.40 -->

What this means here. Chapter 3 owes a time-based split, a cold-start slice built by refitting without the held-out learners, Precision@10 with its K stated, a popularity baseline, and a test unit stated explicitly. The unit matters because the tests recommended against for very large samples are not the same problem as tests over 22 presentations. <!-- C2.41 -->

## 2.7 Documented pitfalls

1. Pure CF fails for new users, sparse data and new items. The proposal's e-commerce hybrid names a latency problem for new items and the unusual user with few overlapping ratings <!-- ledger: degemmis2004hybrid -->; a review defines cold start for users who have rated no or very few items <!-- ledger: roy2022review -->; and the abstract of a systematic review of cold-start approaches (2010 to 2021), the only part of it opened, states that the review includes no experimental benchmark <!-- ledger: panda2022coldstart -->. <!-- C2.42 -->
2. Borrowing movie, music or e-commerce numbers as e-learning targets. The proposal's high-scoring papers are on MovieLens <!-- ledger: huang2019amre --> <!-- ledger: zhang2015hybrid -->, music sessions <!-- ledger: kumar2023session --> and an e-commerce shop <!-- ledger: sabitha2022uirs --> <!-- ledger: degemmis2004hybrid -->. <!-- C2.43 --> Their numbers are evidence about those domains only.
3. Reading 98.98 percent as a recommendation benchmark. <!-- C2.44 --> It is accuracy of URL prediction from a keyword, with a 70:30 split and no ranking <!-- ledger: chaudhary2019elearning -->. <!-- C2.45 -->
4. Reading a paper's summary instead of its table (five cases among the proposal's papers). The AMRE paper is best on accuracy, recall and F1 but last of twelve on precision <!-- ledger: huang2019amre -->, the song RNN's own table shows a stronger baseline than the text implies <!-- ledger: kumar2023session -->, and the CNN course paper's abstract claims a lower course dropout rate although its experiments score Precision@W and Recall@W for predicting truancy (mis-selected) courses, not dropout <!-- ledger: gao2022cnn -->; the proposal's own summaries of the random-plus-kNN paper and the keyword-to-URL paper likewise differ from what those papers measured (search log, discrepancy table). <!-- C2.46 -->
5. Assuming a dataset exists. OULAD is used because none of the nine papers uses a shared public e-learning dataset. <!-- C2.47 --> It is a distance-learning cohort of one institution <!-- ledger: kuzilek2017oulad -->. <!-- C2.76 -->

## 2.9 The gap this thesis fills

Among the sources opened, e-learning recommenders are evaluated mainly by accuracy-type or satisfaction measures on small, private or crawled samples <!-- ledger: dasilva2023educrs --> <!-- ledger: gao2022cnn --> <!-- ledger: ansari2016coders --> <!-- ledger: butmeh2024coldstart --> <!-- ledger: chaudhary2019elearning -->. <!-- C2.65 --> Work on OULAD that I could open uses its behaviour data to predict learner outcomes, not to rank content for learners <!-- ledger: qiu2022behaviour --> <!-- ledger: kuzilek2017oulad -->. <!-- C2.66 --> The closest hybrid built from behaviour logs used the logs of one online-learning platform and reported P@5 only <!-- ledger: sanguino2022course -->. <!-- C2.67 -->

Cold start is a recognised, separately reviewed problem <!-- ledger: panda2022coldstart --> <!-- ledger: roy2022review -->, and e-learning hybrids attack it with ontologies or declared attributes <!-- ledger: jeevamol2021ontology --> <!-- ledger: butmeh2024coldstart -->. <!-- C2.74 --> The abstract of the one systematic review of cold-start approaches that was opened, the only part of it opened, states that it includes no experimental benchmark of the approaches <!-- ledger: panda2022coldstart -->. <!-- C2.68 --> Among the sources opened, none evaluates a behaviour-aware hybrid on a cold-start slice built by refitting the models without the held-out learners. <!-- C2.75 --> Repeated evaluation across course presentations with a stated significance test is absent from the nine reviewed papers as recorded in the notes on them (section 2.6) <!-- ledger: kumar2023session --> <!-- ledger: huang2019amre --> <!-- ledger: gao2022cnn -->, while the evaluation literature asks for splits that follow time, for strong baselines and for care in the choice of significance test <!-- ledger: ji2023leakage --> <!-- ledger: ferrari2021troubling --> <!-- ledger: ihemelandu2023inference -->. <!-- C2.69 -->

The gap is therefore the scarcity of behaviour-aware hybrid recommenders evaluated with a ranking metric on public learner clickstream data, with an explicit cold-start slice and replication across course presentations. It answers RQ0 (hybrid against each single technique, warm and cold) and RQ4 (does an advantage survive significance testing across the 22 presentations); RQ1 and RQ2 draw on the profiling literature <!-- ledger: liang2017profile --> <!-- ledger: hamim2021profile --> <!-- ledger: narimani2024profiling -->; RQ3 (a validation-fitted blend weight) rests on the hybrid literature above <!-- ledger: burke2002hybrid -->. <!-- C2.70 -->

What the gap is not. Hybridisation, CF and CBF, session models and the cold-start problem are all established <!-- ledger: burke2002hybrid --> <!-- ledger: wang2021session --> <!-- ledger: roy2022review -->. <!-- C2.71 --> Nothing here says the hybrid will win: the literature shows a hybrid can lose to pure CF <!-- ledger: degemmis2004hybrid --> and CF was the best single model in the closest analogue <!-- ledger: sanguino2022course -->. <!-- C2.72 --> The claim is about missing evidence in the sources opened. It is defeated by a paper that does what the gap describes; the unopened items in the search log are the first place to look, above all Bhaskaran et al. 2021 (a cluster-based hybrid e-learning recommender, Mathematics, doi 10.3390/math9020197) and Wu et al. 2023 (a hybrid MOOC recommendation with dynamic learner preferences, Applied Sciences, doi 10.3390/app13095548), both refused by the publisher (HTTP 403) and read by title only; I do not know whether either evaluates on a cold-start slice or public learner data. <!-- C2.73 -->

## 2.10 What this chapter established

The chapter fixed the terms, including two corrections to the proposal's Arabic table, and showed that hybrids are common in e-learning but evaluated thinly, that cold start is recognised but not benchmarked, and that current evaluation guidance asks for time-based splits, strong and popularity baselines and an explicit test unit. Chapter 3 needs from it: item-based CF, CBF and a popularity baseline as comparators; Precision@10 named as such; a time-based split; a cold-start slice refitted without the held-out learners; a stated test unit for the significance tests; the OULAD population and licence limits; and the reduction of the behavioural signal to click volume, active days and day order.
